## 1. Project vision and core art-direction statement

*Last Exit* is a lethal televised survival competition taking place in an abandoned urban district that has been repurposed as an arena.

The world is a deteriorating section of a formerly functioning city, situated outside its principal skyscraper district. Its streets, commercial buildings, apartments, public infrastructure, and industrial properties have fallen into economic decline and abandonment. The district has subsequently hosted numerous deadly competitions, so its environment bears evidence of both prolonged neglect and repeated destructive events.

The visual presentation is **top-down, flat 2D, and vector-oriented**, comparable in broad graphical abstraction and camera perspective to *ZombsRoyale.io*. Gameplay takes place predominantly at night. The desired aesthetic combines credible urban decay with bold, sometimes extravagant color and deliberately theatrical lighting.

> **Core art-direction statement:**
> *A derelict urban battleground presented with the dramatic color and visual energy of an extravagant 1980s action spectacle.*

The game must feel abandoned, dangerous, and worn down without becoming drab, monotonous, or visually indistinct. Vibrancy is an affirmative design goal, not merely an occasional exception.

## 3. World identity

### 3.1 A city first, an arena second

The arena was not originally constructed as an arena. Its buildings and infrastructure existed for ordinary civic, residential, and commercial purposes before the district declined and was abandoned; the competition's organizers subsequently acquired or repurposed the area.

The environment must not appear as if every street, alley, building, or obstacle was intentionally designed for combat. Instead, recognizable urban spaces have been adapted, damaged, and exploited for repeated deadly competitions.

### 3.2 Geographic character

The setting is an urban fringe or transitional district outside the city's main skyscraper concentration. Architectural ingredients include:

- Mid-rise offices and apartment buildings
- Old commercial blocks and neighborhood retail strips
- Restaurants, convenience stores, laundromats, and small businesses
- Warehouses and light industrial facilities
- Parking garages and surface parking lots
- Motels and aging hospitality properties
- Municipal buildings and service facilities
- Bus stops, transit corridors, and pedestrian infrastructure
- Alleys, loading areas, service roads, and intersections
- Occasional taller buildings and distant skyscraper silhouettes

The district should resemble an organically developed city with multiple architectural generations rather than a single-purpose industrial complex or uniform collection of ruins.

### 3.3 Abandoned in time

The district has been frozen at the point of its economic collapse, apart from continued deterioration and damage inflicted by successive competitions. Signs, advertising, storefront designs, vehicles, street markings, and architecture preserve evidence of its former life.

Avoid prematurely fixing the district to a single decade or real-world geographic city; its built environment may contain multiple successive generations of architecture and commercial design ([17.3](17.3-product-gameplay.md) #25).

### 3.4 The skyscraper district

The nearby skyscraper district establishes that the arena belongs to a larger metropolis and provides geographical context. The skyscrapers need not dominate the play space or appear continuously in the top-down camera; their visibility and role follow established world scale and camera rules ([25](25-pacing-and-rendering.md)). Do not assume the surrounding city is necessarily prosperous, populated, or accessible ([17.3](17.3-product-gameplay.md) #25).

## 4. Three layers of environmental history

Environmental deterioration must not be implemented as one generic damage treatment. Three conceptually distinct layers coexist ([52](52-map-primitives-and-library.md), #584):

1. **Original construction:** Architectural identity inherited from original function and era—building materials, facade color, commercial branding, roof type and condition, windows, entrances, public vs. private function, and original infrastructure.
2. **Long-term neglect:** Slow deterioration—faded and peeling paint, corrosion, roof damage and water staining, missing fixtures, weathered signage, broken or boarded windows, vegetation growing through pavement, dirt accumulation, exposed structural materials, and improvised repairs. **Decay transforms original colors rather than uniformly eliminating them:** a weathered turquoise storefront may expose rust, darkened metal, and underlying masonry while retaining patches of bright original paint.
3. **Accumulated competition damage:** Violent damage from previous bouts—impact craters, bullet damage, collapsed walls, blast-damaged storefronts, burned vehicles, destroyed barricades, structural breaches, rubble piles, scorch marks, damaged road surfaces, and remnants of previous traps or confrontations. Different events occurred at different times: freshly exposed concrete and recent scorching look distinct from an old breach stained by years of weather. **Not every block is devastated:** relatively intact but abandoned areas establish contrast and make scenes of heavy destruction more impactful.

## 5. Color sources and working palette (`1.0-working`)

### 5.1 Four independent color sources

Color originates from four conceptually independent systems:

- **Urban foundations (`urban`):** asphalt, concrete, brick, stucco, oxidized metal, vegetation, structural shadows.
- **Legacy commercial colors (`legacy`):** restaurant signs, storefronts, murals, advertisements, transit markings, awnings, vending machines, vehicles.
- **Theatrical lighting (`theatrical`):** broad colored illumination or graphic color treatment introduced for atmosphere and composition, with no requirement for a corresponding physical fixture.
- **High-visibility gameplay accents (`gameplay`):** displays, warning signals, hazards, broadcast equipment, extraction markers, hunter identities, competition effects.

Avoid two extremes:
1. **Monochromatic post-apocalyptic drabness:** endless gray-brown environments with little visual personality.
2. **Uniform toy-like saturation:** every surface competing for attention with bright, clean colors.

The target is not a uniformly moderate compromise; subdued and highly saturated regions should coexist deliberately.

### 5.4 Palette philosophy and visual rhythm

Each neighborhood should have an individual palette identity while remaining part of the same city. Base materials typically occupy most of the visual field, but vibrant theatrical lighting may cover large portions of a scene when deliberately composed. There is no fixed percentage cap on saturated pixels.

Vibrancy should have spatial and temporal structure—for example, a muted street opening into a vividly illuminated intersection, an abandoned commercial block punctuated by bright legacy signage, a quiet residential area disrupted by active arena lighting, a dark service alley leading to a brightly marked extraction route, or a combat event that intensifies local theatrical color. Distinguish **color richness** from **visual noise**: strong colors are desirable when they preserve shape recognition, contrast hierarchy, and gameplay legibility. In particular, theatrical lighting must never reduce contrast between walkable surfaces and obstacles.

### 5.5 Selected composition: an ordinary city under extraordinary treatment

**Signature night is the production target.** The selectable themes are color
studies; a global recoloring alone does not establish a neighborhood or finish
the art direction. Default promotion follows [41.2](41.2-art-direction-plan.md).

Compose from large shapes to small ones: street and building masses first,
entrances and cover second, evidence of former life third. Keep walkable ground
quieter than its boundaries. Use broad slate surfaces, warm masonry and patches
of surviving commercial paint, with cobalt treatment interrupted by amber focal
areas. Reserve magenta for a local dramatic counterpoint; applying every accent
to every building erases the contrast that makes the signature work.

A building should have one dominant material and one recognizable commercial
gesture: a broad awning, a sign backing, a loading apron or a distinctive roof
service block. At play scale those survive where tiny lettering and scratch
textures do not. Avoid making all masonry coral or giving every roof a luminous
closed contour. A colored rim belongs to an exposed, deliberately lit edge;
the full structural perimeter must remain readable without it.

Use the four scene identities as compositions within the same city:

| Identity | Material anchor | Local color event | Quiet interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neon Aftermath | Concrete retail frontage and weathered teal sign panels | Cobalt across an intersection, magenta on one frontage, a warm entrance | Slate service alleys between the lit fronts |
| Sodium Graveyard | Brick, oxidized loading bays and road repairs | An amber loading apron with a small turquoise counterpoint | Cooler yards and unlit roof masses |
| Dead Mall District | Cream parapets, faded coral and turquoise commercial remnants | A broken pink sign or a pink wash over one abandoned court | Violet-gray paving with broad empty walking space |
| Broadcast Lockdown | The same underlying civic streets and concrete buildings | A localized deep-blue stage treatment and crisp arena apparatus | Ordinary material remains visible around the intervention |

Broadcast Lockdown is not a permanent gold/cyan outline on every object.
Decorative color may share a hue with gameplay, but never its complete signal:
shape, border, symbol and motion together distinguish a warning or objective.
Active signals keep the strongest local attention. Decorative washes do not
pulse, outline routes or imply a safe zone in the first implementation.

Damage needs a cause and a readable extent. Place a few large stains beneath
drains, weather exposed edges, and group scorch or impact marks around a plausible
event. Keep intact abandoned facades between damaged ones. Surface wear stops
inside the collision silhouette; a jagged breach through that silhouette must
be a real opening. At distant views omit small scars before losing the building's
material mass, entrances or landmark shape. The rendering contract is [29.4](29.4-drawing.md).

### 5.6 Color blocking and architectural graphics

Corey's Marathon-informed additions to the #591 review establish three concrete
principles alongside the Running Man-inspired theatrical lighting:

1. **Large color blocking.** Favor contiguous painted rooftops, sign backings,
   awnings and facade masses over many small colored details. A broad legacy-teal
   rooftop can identify an abandoned commercial block even when its lettering
   disappears at gameplay zoom. Weather it with a few grouped interruptions;
   do not fragment its identifying color field into procedural confetti.
2. **Disciplined complementary contrasts.** Organize a scene around a dominant
   field and a deliberate counterpoint: cobalt with warm orange masonry or rust,
   teal with weathered coral, cool asphalt with ochre markings. The contrast can
   be bold and extensive. Discipline means clear grouping, negative space and
   an attention hierarchy, not a saturation quota. Extra magenta should mark a
   selected focal area rather than become a third competing accent everywhere.
3. **Expressive graphic markings on architecture.** Use broad parking stripes,
   municipal stencils, bay numbers, old advertisements and geometric sign panels
   to explain former use. Give each a recognizable context and sufficient scale.
   Decorative markings carry no required gameplay instruction; avoid cryptic
   glyph systems, fake interaction prompts and warning graphics indistinguishable
   from active hazards. At distant LOD retain the field/silhouette and drop
   unreadable lettering. Arena installations may have selectively crisp graphics
   that contrast with the city's accumulated wear.

The first commercial-block study combines a broad turquoise/teal painted roof,
ochre parking stripes, weathered coral awnings and a cobalt intersection wash.
Working anchors are the existing swatches: asphalt `#49535A`, legacy teal
`#4F8C92`, hot orange `#E87C42`, cobalt `#305EC4`, and broadcast pink
`#D94BC6` (`broadcastPink` in the gameplay group). This differs from theatrical
`showtimeMagenta` (`#B642B6`). The broadcast-pink accent is a candidate whose
decorative use must pass the gameplay-signal separation review in #587, including
the existing Neon Aftermath street marking; color alone does not establish that
separation. These are a composition using the existing palette, not another
registry or an instruction to use all five at equal strength. Credible urban use
and age remain visible beneath the graphic treatment. A player should read the
environment, not decode it.

## 6. Nighttime presentation and theatrical lighting

### 6.1 Night-coded rather than realistically dark

Darkness must not impair gameplay comprehension. Night is communicated through:

- Cool shadow palettes and colored atmospheric treatment
- High-contrast artificial illumination and selective pools of darkness
- Dramatic differences between lit and unlit regions
- Commercial remnants and theatrical accent color

Roads, sidewalks, obstacles, and gameplay-critical objects must remain distinguishable without relying on realistic physical light levels. Large near-black regions are reserved for deliberately unreadable spaces such as inaccessible interiors or deep openings. Avoid reducing the nighttime world to monochromatic blue; warm brick, faded commercial paint, vegetation, orange corrosion, and saturated artificial color retain distinct chromatic identities.

### 6.2 Theatrical lighting as a primary design feature

Colored illumination **does not have to be diegetically justified**. A region can acquire a strong theatrical color identity (cobalt-blue streets with hot orange wall highlights, violet-lit intersections, magenta washes across damaged storefronts, cyan-colored alleys, contrasting blue and red treatment across a combat space) even if no lamp, sign, spotlight, or emitter exists nearby. Do not add fictional light fixtures merely to explain a color wash.

Lighting serves scene identity, local color rhythm, visual depth, perceived volume, dramatic tension, landmark recognition, and character/hazard readability. A saturated wash may cover a large share of the play area if silhouettes and navigation stay legible.

Techniques worth evaluating: flat translucent color regions, colored polygons, simple gradients where supported, alternate material-color variants, region-specific palette transformations, directional edge highlights, selective high-contrast rim treatment, screen-space color overlays, and simplified localized color blending. Which of them fit the renderer and its budget is [29.4](29.4-drawing.md)'s to say (#583).

## 7. Flat top-down readability and gameplay separation

Art decisions must survive flat 2D abstraction viewed from above ([29.4](29.4-drawing.md), #585–#587):

- **Silhouette-first design:** Building footprints, structural walls and entrances, navigable passages, vehicles/wreckage, barricades, rubble/debris, cover, players/hunters, interactive objects, and active hazards must be recognizable primarily through shape and value relationships. Color and detail reinforce identity rather than replacing it.
- **Value separation:** Distinguish roads from sidewalks, walkable surfaces from obstructions, cover from decorative rubble, accessible routes from inaccessible space, characters from the environment, and hazards from harmless lighting using luminance, shape, and perimeter contrast—never hue differences alone.
- **Landmarks at three scales ([54](54-region-types.md)):**
  - *Neighborhood scale:* distinct building types, road patterns, and palette identities.
  - *Block scale:* recognizable landmarks, storefronts, structures, and lighting treatments (damaged gas stations, abandoned theaters, collapsed parking structures, burned-out buses, ruined restaurants, partially destroyed apartment buildings, flooded underpasses, large rooftop signs, wrecked intersections, damaged transit platforms, arena-installed broadcast equipment) defined by distinctive shapes and layouts, not unique colors alone.
  - *Encounter scale:* clear cover, contestant-only squeezes, hazards, obstacles, routes, and combat-relevant objects.
- **Destruction as a visual language (#585):** reusable shape vocabularies that tell the type and extent of damage at gameplay scale: angular breaches for destroyed walls, dark central silhouettes for collapsed roofs, circular or irregular radial marks for craters, large scorch shapes for fire, broken rectangular silhouettes for vehicles, and sparse graphic fractures for damaged pavement. Destruction detail must never misrepresent collision or navigation.
- **Contestants, hunters, hazards, and objectives (#587):** Characters retain consistent silhouette language, controlled outline contrast, distinctive clothing/equipment color regions, and high-value accents across all district palettes and theatrical washes. Specialized hunters (`warden`, `specter`, `striker`) keep individual visual identities that remain legible under any lighting treatment. Hazards, objectives, and interactive objects keep unambiguous visual semantics; decorative lighting must never mimic gameplay warnings.

## 8. What to avoid

- Uniform gray-brown post-apocalyptic environments
- Excessively dark nighttime rendering or making the entire city monochromatic blue
- Requiring every theatrical lighting effect to have a visible physical source or introducing a physically based lighting engine
- Clean, uniformly saturated plastic-looking surfaces
- Procedurally distributing rubble with no structural or historical logic, or making every street equally devastated
- Excessive tiny textures or debris shapes that create visual noise or misrepresent collision/navigation
- Using bright danger colors so widely that actual hazards lose their distinction
- Treating all regions as the same architectural generation
- Building a photorealistic art pipeline incompatible with the flat 2D presentation
- Treating the exploratory working palette hex values as immutable requirements

## 9. Validation criteria

Visual prototypes and art passes are evaluated at actual gameplay camera scale (`playerZoom`, `INSIDE_ZOOM`, and directed LOD views in [25](25-pacing-and-rendering.md)) against ten questions:

1. Does the environment immediately read as a neglected urban district?
2. Is it apparent that the city was originally designed for ordinary life, not exclusively for combat?
3. Does the environment show distinct evidence of aging and violent competition damage?
4. Does the scene convincingly read as nighttime while remaining easy to navigate?
5. Are the colors attractive, distinctive, and occasionally bold?
6. Does theatrical lighting evoke spectacle without requiring physical realism?
7. Can the player quickly identify walls, cover, routes, hazards, and characters?
8. Are district identities visually recognizable without becoming inconsistent with the larger city?
9. Does the environment retain clarity at the actual gameplay camera scale?
10. Does the art direction remain achievable using the established rendering architecture ([29.4](29.4-drawing.md))?

Validation uses representative gameplay-scale screenshots, not only enlarged concept illustrations. To compare one scene across palettes, choose them from the dev view's palette menu or with `?palette=` ([29.4](29.4-drawing.md#palettes)); a replay gives a repeatable scene.

**Creative north star:** *The city supplies the authenticity. The accumulated destruction supplies the history. The theatrical color supplies the spectacle. The flat, readable visual language supplies the gameplay.*
