## Running and inspecting

Run `npm run physics:jelly`, then open `test-results/physics-jelly.html` in a
browser. Select a scene, play at the recorded tick rate, adjust speed, seek
forward/backward or export JSON. Playback uses retained transforms and rule
state, with no solver, server or network requests. The mint polygon joins the
jelly's eight colliding nodes; gold outlines the unchanged collision core.
The blue player ellipse follows compression. This is recorded-scene playback,
not an interactive force editor or the live `/dev/physics` integration lab.

`tools/physics/jelly.mjs` owns synthetic scenes, ordered actions, capture and
the provisional compression rule. `jelly-playback.mjs` owns drawing and seeking;
`jelly-report.mjs` writes the standalone HTML and JSON. No production game,
wire schema, content identity, prediction or recording format changes.

## Provisional lab contract

The jelly has a radius-12 dynamic solid core and eight radius-4 nodes of mass 2
around a radius-24 ring. Native centre springs connect each node to the core
and its next neighbour: stiffness 2,000, damping 60, uncapped. All ordinary
node/core/world collision rules remain active. The core has mass 4. The core
and ring can translate; the ring can deform and rotate. Filled connecting
artwork is not a continuous collider, so small objects could pass between
nodes. This topology is an experiment, not a general soft-body boundary.

The player experiment uses a separate radius-12 solid core with no ring.
Compression never shrinks or changes that collider. Read-only `contacts()`
on the existing Rapier **lab** adapter returns detached, sorted manifold rows
with stable endpoint IDs, a-to-b normals and positive normal impulses converted
to world units. Impulses are accumulated across four substeps per 50 ms tick.
For each observed normal, project external loads onto its axis and take the
smaller opposing total. The largest such load, divided by tick duration,
is a force proxy; it is not pressure per unit area. Contact directions are
normalized before projection. Candidate axes are only observed contact normals,
not every possible direction, so this is not a general multi-contact stress
estimator. Equal 120-degree contacts tie at their observed axes (the 60-degree
bisector is also the same unoriented axis as the 240-degree normal); asymmetric
crowds can behave differently and require a separate reviewed model.

Target compression is force/1,000 capped at 1. Exponential response times are
0.12 s while compressing and 0.25 s while recovering. Six consecutive ticks
at or above 0.7 emit one `lab-crush-threshold` event. Compression below 0.35
clears that signal with `lab-recovered`. A one-sided contact has no opposing
load and cannot trigger compression. The visual ellipse narrows by at most
35% along the compression axis. These are tunable assumptions, not damage,
stun, death or approved combat rules.
The last loaded visual axis is intentionally retained while compression decays;
resetting it on loss of pressure would rotate the recovering ellipse abruptly.

Pinned Rapier 0.21.0 can stop CCD motion against a fixed wall while reporting
zero manifold contact impulse. The diagnostic omits such rows. The crush
witness therefore uses two dynamic box presses; it does not establish
wall/door-driven crush detection. Resolve that missing-load path before any
gameplay adoption. Rotating/changing contact axes and more complex crowds also
need review. No crush sources, team/role policy or live appearance is selected.

## Review of the recorded lab, and the shell in `/dev/physics` (#628)

Corey's review (2026-10-28, #455): the jelly feels good and the player crush does not. Walls touched only the
solid core, and the squish appeared late and unconnected to the wall. That follows from the rule above: it
filters a contact-force proxy (0.12 s to compress, then six ticks above 0.7), so it lags by design, needs
opposing loads, and a fixed wall can report none. The jelly feels connected because its outer nodes are the
colliding bodies. A node ring per player does not fit the P9 envelope (8 x 27 actors is 216 bodies against
128 awake), and the production world allows 8 constraints in total against the jelly's 16.

The interactive lab instead reads a geometric shell from clearance, which is local, immediate and needs no
impulse: see [42.20](42.20-physics-p9.md). It is a lab-only comparison to the recorded proxy, which this page's
playback still shows. Whether any of it reaches the live game, and what a crush does, remain open under #455.

**The jelly in the lab's world.** A scratch probe on the production `PhysicsWorld` (not retained as evidence)
stretched one node 15 units and stepped 160 ticks. A four-node ring with spokes and edges (8 constraints) and an
eight-node ring with spokes only (8 constraints) both read 0.000 radial error (three decimals) at tick 20 and
stayed there at force budgets of 100,000 and 6,000; one run showed 0.001 at tick 160. The stretched start
read 2.5 to 10 units at tick 1 depending on layout and budget. The recorded jelly's 16 joints were refused with
`physics constraint capacity exceeded`. Scene E uses the eight spokes. Its browser test pushes a contestant
into the wall for 90 ticks: the ring deforms (shortest spoke below 21 of 24), no node passes the wall face
beyond 0.1, it recovers to under 0.01 radial error, and an exported recording reruns to identical frames.
That is one wall and one push. Without edge springs the ring has no shear stiffness, so how it behaves
against corners, crates and crowds is untested, and none of it is a capacity or live-game claim.

## PR #507 review response

Review 388 approved the original checkpoint and identified two promotion limits.
The compression proxy now normalizes each contact direction independently of
impulse magnitude, rejects malformed directions/impulses, and explicitly retains
the loaded axis during recovery. Tests verify magnitude invariance and the
recovery orientation. Its contact-normal-only candidate policy remains an
explicit lab limitation; no arbitrary-crowd crush model is promoted here.

Wall crossing diagnostics now use authored allowed half-planes:
`dot(bodyCentre, normal) <= offset`. The current wall has normal `(1,0)` and
offset 215. This removes the implicit right-wall assumption from the sampler.
Mirrored and 90-degree-rotated wall scenes both deform under real contact with
zero reported crossings; moving their diagnostic planes behind the bodies
proves that violations are detected. This still measures sampled centre
half-plane violations, not general continuous containment or finite wall ends.
